---


**Use this when** the week holds less than your list does. Three things get
picked on the grounds that money moves, and they get dates.

## The test, and it is only one test

> Does this end with someone deciding to pay, or actually paying?

That is the whole filter. Not importance, not urgency, not how long you have
been meaning to. A task passes if you can name the person and the moment.

Most of a list fails this. "Redo the pricing page" fails - nobody pays because
a page changed. "Send the proposal sitting in drafts since Tuesday" passes, and
you can name who reads it. The second one is boring and takes nine minutes,
which is exactly why it is still sitting there.

Three, not five. Five is a list, and a list is what you already had.

## How it works

**1. I check the calendar facts before anything else.** What today is, what day
of the week, how many working days are actually left, and whether any date I am
about to hand you is a weekend. This gets checked, never assumed, because a
plan built on the wrong day of the week is wrong in every line.

**2. I sort your whole list into three tiers.**

| tier | meaning |
|---|---|
| `MOVES` | money moves this week because of this |
| `SETS UP` | money moves later, and this is the step that unblocks it |
| `NEITHER` | real work, no payment at the end of it |

**3. I pick three and date them.** Each one gets a day, and the thing that has
to be true for that day to work. If it depends on someone else replying, it
cannot be on Friday.

**4. Everything else gets listed by name under NOT THIS WEEK**, so you can
argue with the cut instead of discovering it on Thursday.

## The trap

A naive version ranks by what you talked about most. You brought up the
portfolio page first and at length, so it goes to the top, and the nine minute
invoice stays unsent for another week. Volume of discussion is not evidence of
payment, and it is often the opposite: the thing you keep talking about instead
of doing is usually the thing that does not pay.

Second trap, the full week. It plans five days when two are already gone to
things you did not mention. The calendar facts get checked, but your existing
commitments do not exist unless you say them, so the plan asks.

## Limits

It cannot see your calendar, your bank, or your invoicing unless you paste
them, so whether a job is really uninvoiced is a guess every time.

It does not know your capacity. Three things on three days assumes those days
are yours.

It cannot tell you the right price or whether a deal is real. It only sorts what
you hand it.

---

## How this one knows what it knows

Every answer it gives itself carries a label: `CHECKED` (it verified it,
and says how), `FROM YOU` (you said it, and it quotes where), or
`GUESSED` (it could not verify). Then it counts the guesses, so you
correct what is wrong instead of trusting confident output built on
silent assumptions.

> Never ask for something you can check.
> Never assume something you cannot check.
